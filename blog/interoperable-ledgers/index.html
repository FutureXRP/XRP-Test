A system of interoperable ledgers.

The BIS named every part of the machine but one.

In forty-five pages on the next-generation monetary system, the world's central banks named the prize, conceded the architecture will be plural, and warned what breaks when ledgers cannot talk to one another. They described, in their own words, the exact world the bridge is built for. They named the asset that sits between the ledgers zero times.

The Living Framework Primary Sources Settlement Architecture June 2026
Editor's note — framework context

This field note is a primary-source read of one document: the Bank for International Settlements Annual Economic Report 2026, Chapter III — "Anchoring trust in money: innovation beyond stablecoins," released 23 June 2026. It is not a price prediction. It is a structural argument about what the report describes, what it concedes, and the one thing it leaves unnamed.

The argument has three movements, the same three this series has used to size the institutional settlement question. Reach: the consortium building the official future covers roughly seven currency areas against a world of some 160 national currencies, and concedes its own work is still a prototype. Access architecture: the report's central distinction — public-permissionless versus private-permissioned — collapses two different questions into one, and in doing so has no name for the design that answers them separately. Atomic settlement: the prize the report defines is a property of co-location, and every scenario it models lives inside a single connected system. None models the seam between systems that do not share a sovereign.

The central caveat, stated once, plainly, and never softened: the report does not mention XRP, Ripple, or the XRP Ledger. Not once. Every application here is interpretive. This is the most authoritative entry yet in the pattern Field Note 14 catalogued — the institutions describing the problem are not writing about the asset built to solve it. And the report is not a bullish document. It hardens the incumbent roadmap: Project Agorá is now a demonstrated prototype rather than a proposal, stablecoins are formally downgraded, and the central-bank-anchored unified ledger is restated as the destination. The bridge argument lives in the seam the report leaves open. It does not live in anything the report endorses.

Once a year the central banks' central bank publishes a flagship report, and once a year a chapter of it tells you where the official monetary system intends to go next. The 2026 chapter is the most complete statement of that destination the BIS has produced. It defines the prize precisely. It concedes the architecture will not be one ledger but many. It warns, in unusually plain language, what happens when value tries to cross between ledgers that were never built to communicate. And then it describes its endgame in six words worth holding onto.

"A system of fully interoperable ledgers."

— BIS Annual Economic Report 2026, Chapter III, on how the unified ledger may be implemented

Read that as what it is: the institution most committed to the two-tier system, describing its own future as a plurality of ledgers that must be made to interoperate. Not one chamber. Many. The fragmented, multi-ledger world is no longer a scenario this series has to argue for against the official view. It is now the official view's own design assumption. Everything below follows from taking the report at its word.

Section 01 — What the report actually says

Start with the honest framing, because the bull case for the bridge is stronger when the report is described accurately than when it is oversold. This is a preserve-and-upgrade document. Its unifying principle is that technology should serve the public-good functions of money rather than undermine them, and it treats tokenisation as an evolution of the existing system, not a new form of money. Against last year's corpus, three things have hardened.

What hardened — Annual Economic Report 2026 vs the prior BIS corpus
Project Agorá — status demonstrated prototype
Agorá legal finding — tokenisation changes nature of deposits/reserves it does not
Stablecoin issuers — classified (Ch. II) as a new NBFI species
Stablecoin designs resemble ETF shares, not money
Unified ledger — restated destination (CB reserves + bank money + bonds) unchanged
Endgame architecture — newly phrased as interoperable ledgers
Agorá was a proposal in the 2023 Blueprint and the 2025 unified-ledger chapter. In 2026 it is reported as a working prototype that settled cross-currency payment-versus-payment, all-or-nothing — with the report adding that any move to production would require further measures. The roadmap is firmer, not softer.

So the report is not a gift. It is a more confident statement of the incumbent plan than any prior year, and it arms the standard rebuttal to public networks with more force than before. The opening it leaves is not a weakness in the plan. It is a seam in the plan's geometry — and the seam is the same one this series has circled since Part I.

Section 02 — Movement I: seven currency areas, a hundred and sixty roads

The report's evidence that the architecture is feasible is Project Agorá: seven central banks and more than forty regulated private institutions, testing a shared cross-border platform that settles tokenised commercial-bank money against jurisdiction-specific tokenised central-bank reserves. It works. The prototype settled atomically across currencies. This note does not dispute the achievement. It asks a narrower question the report does not: how much of the world does the consortium actually reach?

Reach of the official platform — Project Agorá
Central banks participating 7
Distinct currency areas represented ~7
Private institutions participating 40+
Production-ready today no — prototype
Actively circulating national currencies worldwide ~160
The "40+" are private banks, not currencies. The seven central banks span roughly seven currency areas — only Banque de France carries the euro — so the currency reach and the institution count are different numbers. The ~160 figure is the count of actively circulating national currencies, a round directional number rather than the full ISO 4217 register. Both are used here as orders of magnitude, not precise tallies.

The headline "forty" is private banks; it is not a currency count, and several readings of the project quietly inflate it into one. The currency reality is roughly seven areas. Set that against the order of magnitude that matters for cross-border settlement, and the shape of the problem appears.

Currency areas wired by the official platform vs the currency universe
7 vs ~160
The major-currency core is exactly where a direct tokenised pair will exist, atomic settlement will be available, and a neutral bridge has the least to do. The long tail — thin emerging-market-to-emerging-market corridors the consortium has no commercial reason to wire — is where pre-funding costs are highest and where the platform reaches nothing.

This is the load-bearing distinction, and it is also where the argument must be disciplined. The strong claim is narrow and survives scrutiny: a permissioned consortium of major central banks will build the corridors among themselves and will not build the long tail, because the long tail has no member willing to underwrite it. The fragile claim — that XRP "replaces the settlement layer" — does not survive, and the report itself arms the rebuttal to it. The reach argument is about the residual the consortium leaves on the table, not about displacing the core it is busy occupying.

Two conditions would falsify even the narrow claim, and they should be named. First, if the consortium's own "interoperable networks" onboard the long tail faster than expected — through a permissioned bridging mechanism rather than a neutral public one — the seam closes without a neutral asset ever entering it. Second, if a small set of wholesale-CBDC corridors expands to cover the corridors that matter, the residual shrinks to a remainder too small to price. Neither has happened. Both are live risks, and the framework is conditional on them not resolving against it.

Section 03 — Movement II: the binary the report won't split

The report's organising distinction is public-permissionless versus private-permissioned. Everything hangs from it: stablecoins live on the public-permissionless side and inherit its problems — validator rents, congestion, gas fees, fragmentation across base and secondary networks — while the preferred unified ledger lives on the private-permissioned side and inherits its virtues, governance and integrity. The trouble is that this single axis is doing the work of two.

Who validates — open and anonymous, or curated and identifiable — is one question. Who accesses — anyone, or an approved list — is a different question. The report binds them together, and by binding them it imports the economic pathologies of Ethereum and Solana (rents, gas, congestion, the layer-1/layer-2 split it documents at length) as if they were the necessary price of public access. They are not. They are the price of open validation. The proof that the report has conflated the two is in its own taxonomy.

The report's consensus-mechanism table lists proof-of-work, proof-of-stake, and delegated-stake — Bitcoin, Ethereum, BNB Chain, Tron. The XRP Ledger's model, a curated Unique Node List of identifiable validators with no block rewards and no gas, is the only row above that is curated in validation and public in access — and it is the only one absent from the taxonomy entirely. The report's whole critique of public networks is aimed at the rent-and-congestion economics of open validation. It is not aimed at, and does not describe, a public-access ledger that does not use open validation. That design is not rebutted. It is simply not in the map.

The report knows the hybrid exists. In its endnotes it names "public permissioned networks" and a "network of networks," acknowledges that designs increasingly blend the two sides — and then states it will not treat them in depth. The walled-garden risk it concedes for purely permissioned platforms is the giveaway: gating access requires a gatekeeper, and at cross-border scale the gatekeeper is the club of major central banks. Public access is the only architecture under which the long tail needs no one's approval to settle. On that axis, the permission question becomes the addressable-market question.

Honesty requires naming what cuts the other way, because this is the movement most vulnerable to overreach. The XRP Ledger's base layer is transparent, and institutions do not want their wholesale flows publicly legible — a concern the report flags directly for public networks. The trusted-validator list is attacked from both sides at once: as too centralised by the decentralisation camp, and as too public by the BIS camp. The defensible position is architectural, not ideological — claim the quadrant the binary has no name for, and do not overclaim pure decentralisation the design does not provide.

Section 04 — Movement III: the prize is co-location, not the bridge

The report's prize is precise: atomic settlement in central-bank money on a shared ledger, with the cross-border case being payment-versus-payment — two cash legs in two currencies, locked and swapped all-or-nothing. This is worth stating exactly, because precision here is where the series earns its credibility.

Precision — PvP is not DvP

The cross-border prize the report demonstrates is payment-versus-payment (two currency legs). That is distinct from the delivery-versus-payment (asset-versus-cash) case that Field Note 13 found to be unsplittable. Both are atomic; both are co-located. The distinction matters because the argument below turns on where the two legs live, not on which kind of swap it is.

Atomicity is a property of co-location. Two assets sit on one ledger; the protocol locks both and exchanges them in a single indivisible step. That is precisely how Agorá settles, and precisely how the XRP Ledger's own decentralised exchange and auto-bridging settle two assets issued on it. The bridge function — source currency into a neutral asset and out the far side, two hops through a volatile intermediary — is a different mechanism. Conflating them is the most common error in this debate, and the series should not make it. The report's atomic prize is co-location. It is not the bridge. Which raises the question the report never asks: what settles between two ledgers that do not share a central bank?

Look at how the report models the future and the omission becomes structural. It builds three reserve scenarios for a stablecoin economy. Every one of them lives inside a single connected system.

The reserve scenarios the BIS models — and the one it does not
Scenario 1 — Issuer holds wholesale bank deposits
Retail deposits are displaced and recycled as wholesale deposits inside the same banking system. A compositional shift in one set of balance sheets. Everything settles within a single connected ledger of claims.
Models settlement inside one system
Scenario 2 — Issuer holds short-dated government bills
The issuer rotates deposits into treasury bills bought from banks and other non-banks. Liquidity metrics move; high-quality liquid assets shift hands. Still one domestic system, one sovereign, one connected set of rails.
Models settlement inside one system
Scenario 3 — Issuer holds central-bank reserves
The most liquid case: reserves move from banks to the issuer's central-bank account. The stablecoin becomes a near-substitute for central-bank money. One central bank, one reserve system, one connected ledger.
Models settlement inside one system
The scenario absent from the chapter — settlement between unconnected ledgers
Two ledgers, two sovereigns, no shared central bank, no co-located tokenised pair, value that must cross the boundary all-or-nothing without principal risk. This is the corridor where pre-funding is most expensive and Herstatt risk is worst. It is the precise function a neutral bridge performs. The report models it nowhere.
✓ The seat this series has always been about — still empty in the model
The report models everything inside a perimeter and is silent on the asset that moves value between perimeters. That is not an oversight to mock — it is the exact shape of the gap. The prize the report defines is co-location; the function it does not model is the bridge.

So the bridge claim must split cleanly into two, and only the second is about the token. Rail value: the XRP Ledger can deliver the report's own atomic prize for two assets issued on it — the same structural achievement as Agorá, on a public-access rail rather than a gated one. Token value: the asset XRP captures value as the neutral liquidity bridge for long-tail corridors where no co-located tokenised pair exists, and — contingently — as the inter-ledger bridge if the world fragments into multiple non-interoperable regional unified ledgers. The concession that keeps this honest: inside the dense permissioned core, where a direct tokenised pair already exists, the bridge asset has no structural role. Its domain is the seam, not the core.

Section 05 — The concession, in their own words

Return to the six words. The report does not describe one unified ledger as the destination; it describes a unified ledger that may be implemented as a system of interoperable networks — and, in the body, as "a system of fully interoperable ledgers." It calls interoperability money's connective tissue. It then spends pages on what happens when that tissue is absent: a token on one chain is not equivalent to the same-named token on another, because they sit on separate ledgers that do not natively communicate; bridges between them carry operational risk; and sending value across them can fail outright, risking, in the report's own phrase, "permanent loss."

Hold those three admissions together. The future is plural ledgers. Interoperability is the connective tissue that future depends on. And the mechanisms that currently provide it across ledgers are fragile enough to lose funds permanently. The report has described the precise conditions under which a neutral, ledger-agnostic settlement asset has a job — and has named everything in the machine except that asset.

The dollarisation section is where the unnamed antidote is most visible. The report lays out, in detail, how foreign dollar-stablecoins erode monetary sovereignty in emerging economies, link crypto rails to FX markets, and slip past capital controls — and its proposed remedy is sound domestic frameworks plus the central-bank-anchored unified ledger. It never once considers that a neutral, non-sovereign bridge asset could let an emerging economy settle cross-border without dollarising. The argument that the bridge is the antidote to dollar hegemony sits fully assembled in the report's own evidence. The report declines to make it. Underneath, it cites a European central banker's "monetary sovereignty" address — the same sovereignty anxiety driving Europe's stablecoin and licensing regime.

One more thing the report does, quietly, is deflate the instrument it spends the most pages on. Its own US-calibrated model finds the net medium-term output effect of widespread stablecoin adoption is slightly negative — the drag on bank funding and lending outweighs the fiscal-space benefit from issuer demand for government bills — and calls the effect "quantitatively modest" even at one, two, and three trillion dollars of market value. The volume story shrinks under the same scrutiny.

The model's margins: public debt/GDP 122%→175%, treasury-bill share 18%→24%, half of stablecoins held abroad. The BIS is not arguing stablecoins are the settlement future; it is arguing, with its own model, that they fall short of money and that the macro payoff is small-to-negative. This is direct support for the float-insufficiency case in Field Note 16 — made by the BIS, against stablecoins, from the inside.

Section 06 — What this note does not claim

The report does not endorse a neutral bridge asset, does not mention the one this series is about, and should not be read as quietly blessing it. It is a central-bank document arguing for a central-bank-anchored future, and it arms the strongest objections to public networks more thoroughly than any prior edition. The argument here is strictly structural: the report describes a problem whose shape matches the asset, while naming the asset nowhere — exactly the pattern Field Note 14 documented across the BIS, IMF, NY Fed, TBAC, SEC, and DTCC corpus.

The deepest objection the report raises is integrity at the edge: unhosted wallets, pseudonymity, and the know-your-customer gap on public chains. It is the report's single strongest argument against a public-access settlement rail, and it should be conceded as strong rather than waved away. The honest rejoinder is that integrity is enforceable at the regulated on- and off-ramp and the intermediary layer — where identified institutions interface with the public — rather than baked into the rail itself; the report's own "integrity by design" passage describes screening embedded at the transaction interface, not in the base protocol. That is a real answer, but it is the place the public-access case is most exposed, and the series should keep it flagged as such.

So the falsification conditions, stated plainly, as this series always states them. The bridge thesis weakens materially if any of the following resolve against it: the consortium's interoperable networks onboard the long tail through a permissioned bridging mechanism, closing the seam before a neutral asset enters it; integrity-at-edge proves regulatorily fatal to a public-access rail for wholesale settlement; or a permissioned, central-bank-issued settlement instrument expands to cover the corridors that matter, shrinking the addressable residual to a remainder too small to price. None has happened. All are live. The conclusion is conditional on them not resolving — and the report, read honestly, does not resolve them against the thesis. It describes the world in which the thesis is true, and declines to name the asset.
