Two kinds of batch.

One waits so trades can cancel out. The other refuses to wait at all.

The IMF says the way to make instant settlement affordable is to wait. The XRP Ledger just switched on the tool for not waiting. Both are called a batch — and they mean opposite things.

The Living Framework Primary Sources Settlement Architecture October 9, 2026
Editor's note — what this is

Two things landed a day apart this week, and they point in opposite directions. Yesterday, October 8, the International Monetary Fund published its chapter on tokenization in the October 2026 Global Financial Stability Report. Today, October 9, the XRP Ledger's Batch upgrade went live on the main network.

Both use the word "batch." They do not mean the same thing. One batch slows settlement down so that trades can cancel each other out. The other makes several transfers happen at the same instant, or not at all. Whether the market picks one or blends the two is the question this series has been circling since Part II.

What the IMF said

The chapter is called "Scaling Tokenization: New Efficiencies, New Vulnerabilities." It is not about XRP. That is what makes it useful: it is the plumbing described by the people responsible for it.

Three findings matter for this series.

  1. No settlement money works across tokenized platforms yet. When a tokenized bond changes hands, something has to pay for it on the same ledger. Central bank money, bank deposits, and stablecoins each do that job somewhere today. None does it safely across every platform. The IMF lists the lack of a safe on-chain settlement asset as one of four things holding tokenization back, next to legal certainty, regulatory clarity, and networks that cannot talk to each other.
  2. Instant settlement costs more cash, not less. The IMF says all-or-nothing settlement removes the risk that one side fails to deliver. It also says this raises the amount of money firms must have ready during the day, because everything has to be funded up front.
  3. The market is small and split up. Tokenized assets total about $65 billion, roughly 0.20 percent of outstanding US Treasuries. The busiest corner is tokenized repo, short-term lending against collateral, at about $303 billion a day.

The XRP Ledger appears once, in a chart of the networks that carry tokenized value. It sits alongside Ethereum, Solana, Avalanche, Stellar, and BNB Chain. The text does not give its share.

Netting needs time. You have to wait for offsetting trades to arrive before you can cancel them against each other. Settle each trade the instant it happens and there is nothing to cancel it against.

The IMF agrees. In a section on tokenized repo, it says that as settlement moves closer to real time, the share of trades that can be netted could fall substantially, leaving dealers needing more cash and more balance sheet. That is the argument of Part II of this series, in the IMF's own words.

Then comes its answer. Use software to hold trades for a short window, group them, net them, and settle the remainder. The IMF calls this programmable batching and is plain about the price: it comes at the cost of settlement speed. It cites research on stock trading suggesting much of the saving survives even with windows as short as one hour, when a central clearinghouse sits in the middle.

The IMF's batch is a step back from instant settlement. It keeps the ledger and gives up the instant.

— The first kind of batch

The XRP Ledger's batch: all or nothing, live today

The XRP Ledger's Batch upgrade went live today, Friday, October 9, 2026, at 2:46 PM UTC (9:46 AM Central). Its companion security fix switched on 34 minutes earlier, at 2:12 PM UTC.

The upgrade, known as BatchV1_1 or XLS-56, lets an account bundle up to eight transactions into one. In its all-or-nothing mode, every transaction in the bundle succeeds together or the whole bundle fails as if it never happened.

That is the property settlement people care about. The bond moves if and only if the payment moves. Field Note 13 covered why that link cannot be split.

Three points of honesty about the upgrade:

  • It is not the ledger's first atomic tool. The XRP Ledger has been able to swap one asset for another in a single step since 2012. Batch extends that to multi-step settlements involving several parties.
  • It took two tries. A serious flaw was found in the first version in February 2026, while validators were still voting. It never reached the live network and no funds were at risk. The corrected version was first on track for September 29, then slipped when validator support dipped and the two-week voting clock reset.
  • The vote held, but only just in time. An upgrade needs more than 80 percent validator support for two full weeks. As of October 6 the companion security fix showed 100 percent, and both amendments activated on schedule this afternoon.

So the XRP Ledger's batch is the opposite of the IMF's. Nothing waits and nothing is netted. Everything in the bundle settles in the same moment.

A possible blend: net first, then settle the remainder atomically

The two batches do not have to be rivals. A clearinghouse could keep netting and then settle whatever is left in one all-or-nothing step.

Take $1 trillion of trades. If netting removes 98 percent, $20 billion still has to be paid. Netting has shrunk the bill. It has not decided what the bill is paid in.

Inside one country, one currency, and one clearinghouse, the answer is easy: central bank money. That is how the US Treasury market settles today, and it is where this blend is most likely to take hold. No bridge asset is needed there.

The blend gets harder as it stretches. Part II of this series laid out why: netting depends on a waiting window, and the shorter the window, the fewer trades cancel out. A market that runs around the clock, across several ledgers and currencies, has shorter windows and no single clearinghouse sitting in the middle of all of it.

So this note treats the blend as possible, and likely in the places that already net well. It is much less clear that it can cover value moving between ledgers and currencies. That leftover is where a neutral asset would have work to do.

The concession, at full strength

It would be easy to read this week's chapter as the IMF validating the XRP thesis. It does not, and this series does not get to pretend otherwise.

  • The IMF never considers a bridge asset. Its definitions set aside protocol tokens and crypto assets such as Bitcoin and Ether. XRP, Ripple, and RLUSD are not mentioned. The settlement options it weighs are central bank money, tokenized bank deposits, and stablecoins.
  • Its best case settles in central bank money. In the IMF's optimistic scenario, networks connect through common standards and pay each other in central bank money. No neutral asset is needed in that picture.
  • Its likely case is private plumbing. The IMF expects tokenization to grow first inside permissioned networks run by banks, custodians, and market utilities, largely invisible to investors. It notes that most tokenized repo today is between affiliated firms.
  • The market is voting for netting right now. The US clearinghouse for Treasuries and repo clears more than $12 trillion a day, up 165 percent since regulators first proposed mandatory clearing. That is money moving toward the netted system, not away from it.
  • A tool is not demand. Batch makes atomic multi-party settlement cleaner on the XRP Ledger. It does not make any institution choose it, and it does not make them pay in XRP. A bond can settle against a stablecoin such as RLUSD on the XRP Ledger with XRP covering only the network fee. There are three separate steps: the ledger can do it, institutions actually use it, and that use creates demand for XRP. Today's activation closed the first — the step the other two could not begin without.

What the chapter does give the thesis is narrower and still real. The IMF confirms that no settlement money yet works safely across tokenized platforms. It confirms that instant settlement destroys netting. And it expects many separate ledgers for years, which means something has to carry value between them.

The thesis remains conditional. If settlement stays batched and netted inside private networks, a bridge asset has little to do. If it moves to instant settlement across many ledgers, the gap the IMF describes has to be filled by something.

Why the odds are better than the chapter reads

Hold the concessions above in one hand. Now hold the other side, because a conditional thesis is not a lottery ticket, and the conditions here are not hypothetical.

  • The IMF didn't describe a long shot. It described a vacancy. Of the four things the Fund says are holding tokenization back, three — legal certainty, regulatory clarity, interoperability — are problems governments and standards bodies grind through. The fourth, a safe settlement asset that works across platforms, is a seat waiting for an occupant. The chapter establishes that the seat exists and is empty. That is not a reason for pessimism about the asset built for it.
  • XRP is not auditioning from zero. It is the only neutral asset already doing cross-border settlement in production at scale: more than $35 billion annualized through ODL as of Q1 2026, growing 41 percent year over year, across 70-plus corridor pairs covering roughly 80 percent of major global remittance routes. Every other candidate for the bridge seat is a whitepaper or a pilot. This one has a decade of flow behind it.
  • The infrastructure keeps compounding in one direction. A native exchange since 2012. Settlement finality in seconds. Fees burned rather than paid to validators — the zero coordination premium of Field Note 18. And as of this afternoon, atomic all-or-nothing multi-party settlement. Add the regulatory layer: an OCC trust-bank charter conditionally approved, a Federal Reserve payment-access application in process, and more litigated legal clarity in the United States than any other major digital asset. Each piece makes the next institution's decision easier.
  • The IMF's best case has the worst track record. Its optimistic scenario — every network linked by common standards, settling in central bank money across borders — is the scenario official bodies have been promising for a decade and missing. The FSB has already conceded the G20's 2027 cross-border targets will not be met. The world where coordination arrives slowly and unevenly is the world we are actually in, and it is precisely the world where a neutral bridge earns its keep.
  • This series' own math does not price a moonshot. In the Part VI adoption framework, the scenarios in which XRP captures or shares the settlement hub role carry a combined 35 to 55 percent probability. That is not one in a million. That is a coin flip whose payoff structure the next section prices.

What the formula says the price must do

Readers of this series know what comes next, because the math has not changed since Part I. If institutions do settle through XRP at size, the market must be deep enough to absorb the peak ticket without breaching slippage tolerance — and depth is a function of price. The square-root market impact law makes that requirement exact.

The square-root law, inverted — Part I
slippage = σ × √(ticket ÷ volume)
required price = ticket × (σ ÷ tol)² ÷ turnover ÷ supply
Set slippage equal to the institution's tolerance, note that daily volume = turnover × price × supply, and solve for price. Every variable is observable or a stated assumption.

Read the table the way the series always has: not as a forecast but as a consistency check. The first row says remittance-scale settlement needs a price in the teens — an order of magnitude from here, not a fantasy. Depth is not a vibe. It is purchased with market capitalization, and the formula says exactly how much.

The question that remains

The IMF has described the liquidity problem. The XRP Ledger now has a live tool for atomic settlement. Neither one shows that XRP will become a settlement asset.

The financial system may not have to choose between netting and instant settlement. It may use both. But after trades are netted, something is still owed. After ledgers are connected, value still has to cross between them. Someone has to supply that liquidity, in some asset.

Central bank money can handle obligations inside one country. Tokenized deposits and stablecoins can handle payments inside their own networks. Connected systems may link some networks with no bridge asset at all. But if a meaningful share of the market needs neutral liquidity between networks — and the IMF's own chapter says nothing currently provides it — XRP is the best-positioned candidate for the job, and the only one already doing a version of it in production at tens of billions of dollars a year.

Batch does not prove the thesis. It arms it. The capability question is now closed; the adoption question is open; and the price question, if adoption comes, has an exact answer — the table above. The next evidence will not come from an amendment vote. It will come from the settlement flows, and the calendar that forces them is already printed.

Framework status — what this note adds

What's new: The netting-versus-instant-settlement tension from Part II now has the IMF's own confirmation — as settlement moves toward real time, nettable trades fall and funding needs rise — plus the IMF's proposed middle path, programmable batching. The atomic-settlement capability Field Note 13 argued is irreducible is no longer pending on the XRP Ledger: it is live. And the Part I square-root law now has a scenario price table anchored to today's supply and price, consistent with the live calculator and terminal.

The affirmative case, stated plainly: The seat the IMF describes is empty, the asset built for it is the only neutral candidate with production flow behind it, and this series' own Part VI framework puts the hub-role scenarios at a combined 35–55 percent — a coin flip with the payoff structure the price table makes explicit, not a long shot.

The honest limits, kept in plain sight: The IMF chapter never mentions XRP, Ripple, or RLUSD. Its preferred settlement assets are central bank money, tokenized deposits, and stablecoins. A live amendment is capability, not adoption, and not demand. Every link drawn here is this series' reading, not the IMF's claim.
