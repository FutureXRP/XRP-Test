The toll and the cargo.

A map of where token demand actually comes from.

A blockchain can move a trillion dollars a year without its coin being worth a trillion dollars. Whether the coin benefits depends on one thing: does the coin carry the money, or does it just collect the toll?

The Living Framework Token Design Methodology October 23, 2026
Editor's note — what this is

This note maps token design, not current scale and not expected returns. It is a consistency check: the series has spent nineteen notes sizing how much value could flow through XRP. This one asks the question underneath all of them — why a flow should create demand for the token at all, rather than just for the network — and applies the same test to every major utility token.

The one question

A blockchain can move a trillion dollars a year without its coin being worth a trillion dollars. Whether the coin benefits depends on one thing: does the coin carry the money, or does it just collect the toll?

Think of a highway. Some tokens are the toll booth: every truck pays a few cents, no matter what it hauls. Other tokens are the cargo itself: the value being shipped sits inside them for the length of the trip. A busy highway makes the toll booth a little richer. A busy highway full of your cargo is a different economic event entirely.

This Field Note sorts the major utility tokens by that question. It is not a price ranking. It is a map of where demand comes from in each design, so holders can tell adoption headlines that touch the token from adoption headlines that only touch the network.

The four jobs a token can do

Every utility token does some mix of four jobs.

  1. Carry the principal (cargo). The full payment value passes through the token. $10 million in dollars becomes XRP, crosses the ledger, and becomes $10 million in pesos. Demand scales with payment value.
  2. Pay the fee (toll). A $10 million tokenized bond changes hands, and the token pays a fraction of a cent to process it. Demand scales with the number of transactions, not their size.
  3. Secure or back the system (deposit). Tokens are locked for staking, validator bonds, collateral, or account reserves. Demand scales with how much the system needs held in place — and when collateral requirements are set as a fraction of the value at risk, this job can scale with principal too.
  4. Unlock a service (key). Governance votes, oracle data, storage, compute, platform access. Demand depends on that service's own economics, often only loosely linked to the money on the network.

The cargo job is the only one where demand scales with payment value by construction. The deposit job can get there, but only if someone — a regulator, a protocol, a counterparty — sets the collateral requirement in proportion to the money moving. The toll and the key almost never do: where a fee is set as a percentage of value, revenue scales with principal — but it is still money spent through the token, not principal held in it.

One caution before the map. Almost any coin can pay for something priced in itself; you can send someone ETH. The real question is narrower: does the network's intended institutional use require the coin to carry the value, or can the institution move the money in some other asset and only touch the coin for fees?

The map

XRP and XLM stand apart on this map because their ledgers have native mechanisms that can route cross-currency payments through the native token itself. Other networks can support currency exchange — through applications, liquidity pools, market makers, and interoperable assets — but the native coin is not necessarily part of settlement. Everything else on this list is mainly a toll, a deposit, or a key, even when it can technically be sent as payment.

This maps what each design intends. It is not a claim that any of these uses runs at institutional scale today. A network can sit in the toll column and still see heavy institutional use; it just means that use is not automatically token demand. And a network can sit in the cargo row while its cargo mechanism goes mostly unused — which is why this note ends with tripwires rather than conclusions.

Close-ups

Six networks matter most for institutional finance. Here is what each one's coin actually does when a large payment moves.

XRP (XRP Ledger): cargo, by design. The ledger's exchange auto-bridges: when a token-to-token trade executes, the protocol automatically routes it through XRP whenever that path gives a better rate (XRPL auto-bridging). Cross-currency payments do not auto-bridge by default, but pathfinding can select an XRP leg when it is the cheapest route — so XRP participation is built in, automatic where it wins on price, and never guaranteed. Issued tokens can also move directly, touching XRP only for fees. It is the clearest case of a coin built to be the cargo — and the share of trades actually routing through XRP is a measurable number, which Observatory No. 4 tracks as a live metric.

XLM (Stellar): cargo where liquidity exists, toll everywhere. XLM can bridge currencies through path payments, and every account must hold a small XLM reserve and pay XLM fees (Stellar docs). But nothing requires Stellar volume to route through XLM, and the network's flagship institutional uses — USDC, tokenized funds, and the DTCC registry connection (DTCC's tokenization pilot entered limited production in July 2026; DTC-tokenized assets on Stellar are targeted for mid-2027 — DTCC announcement) — move issued assets directly, with XLM collecting the toll.

XDC (XDC Network): toll and deposit, with optional cargo. XDC pays gas and secures validators (XDC FAQ). A $100 million tokenized trade instrument can move on XDC without anyone buying $100 million of XDC. Circle launched native USDC and CCTP V2 on XDC in September 2025, giving the network a non-XDC settlement asset (Circle). To be clear about what the design permits: XDC is a capable payment coin — seconds to finality, negligible fees — and as an EVM network it can settle DvP atomically, composing the asset leg and the cash leg into one all-or-nothing transaction (a generality the XRP Ledger only reached with October's Batch amendment). What the design does not do is put XDC in the cash leg by default: that seat is being filled by USDC and tokenized deposits, and XDC earns the cargo label the moment institutions choose it as the settlement asset — the same test this series applies to XRP on rails where RLUSD settles the cash leg today.

HBAR (Hedera): toll and deposit. Hedera prices fees in US dollars and collects them in HBAR; HBAR also secures the network (Hedera FAQ). A billion-dollar tokenized fund can run on Hedera while HBAR pays only the service fees.

LINK (Chainlink): key. LINK pays for oracle and cross-chain (CCIP) services and is staked by node operators. Three demand channels, all service-sized: fees denominated and paid in LINK, fees paid in alternative assets (CCIP accepts some non-LINK fee tokens — Chainlink docs), and staking. CCIP can move billions in other assets without that value ever being LINK.

ETH and SOL: toll, deposit, and collateral. Both need their coin for gas and staking, and ETH is heavily used as DeFi collateral. A $50 million tokenized security sold on Ethereum does not require $50 million of ETH, and the same holds for SOL on Solana (Solana payments docs). Their institutional volume and their coin's demand are related, but not the same number. One honest wrinkle — DEX routers do push principal through these coins — is taken up in the concession section below.

What $1 trillion a year does to each model

Send the same $1 trillion a year through three designs and the demand mechanisms are not just different sizes — they are different kinds of number. These are illustrations, not forecasts.

A. Cargo — XRP as the bridge Carries all $1T of principal A standing float: $20B–$50B of the token held in place at all times
B. Toll — XDC or HBAR fees only Pays a per-transaction fee An annual spend: on the order of $10K–$1M per year, with only a tiny working balance ever held
C. Key — LINK via CCIP Pays for the messaging service; the $1T moves in other assets Set by service pricing and staking, not by the $1T

Gate 1 — the quantity identity
working float  =  annual flow  ÷  turns per year
$1T ÷ 50 turns = $20B  ·  $1T ÷ 20 turns = $50B  ·  at 100 turns: $10B  ·  at 10 turns: $100B

How Scenario A is sized — call it Gate 1. The series uses the quantity identity: value held equals annual flow divided by how many times that value turns over. Observatory No. 1 puts the defensible institutional velocity for XRP market makers at roughly 20 to 50 turns a year (the constraint-by-constraint reasoning — what atomic settlement relaxes and what it cannot — is there). Under that modeled turnover assumption, the implied average working inventory is $1 trillion ÷ 50 = $20 billion to $1 trillion ÷ 20 = $50 billion of XRP sitting in the pipes at any moment — working inventory, which can be met partly from existing holdings or borrowed XRP, not necessarily net new buying. The conditionality cuts both ways: at 100 turns a year the float is $10 billion, at 10 turns it is $100 billion — which is what makes the velocity range the model's most consequential input.

Why would anyone hold inventory for a week or two when the payment itself settles in seconds? Because settlement speed and inventory turnover are different variables. A market maker's float is sized by position limits, buffer capacity across market conditions, and the timing mismatch between inbound and outbound flows — not by ledger close time. Fast settlement lets the float be smaller than a nostro account; it does not let it be zero. Treat 20–50× as what it is: a modeled range grounded in Observatory No. 1's constraint analysis, not an observed institutional benchmark. It is exactly the kind of number the tripwires below exist to validate.

How Scenario B is sized. Tolls scale with transaction count, not value — so count the transactions. $1 trillion at an average ticket of $10,000 is 100 million transactions; at Hedera's published $0.0001 transfer fee (fee schedule), that is about $10,000 a year. Price the toll more generously — say 0.0001% of value — and it is still only about $1 million a year. Low fees are a selling point for adoption; they are also why enormous fee-only volume spends almost nothing on the token. And a fee is held for minutes, not weeks: the toll model's standing balance rounds to zero. (One real difference inside the toll column: the XRPL destroys its fees, while most networks pay them to validators, who can sell them. A burned toll is a slow supply sink; a recycled toll is not even that.)

Comparing like with like. As a flow: $1 trillion a year passes through the token in the cargo model, against $10,000 to $1 million a year spent on it in the toll model — six to eight orders of magnitude apart. But throughput is not demand: the same XRP is bought, used, and reused many times over, and an institution can source its float from another institution without any net new holding. That is exactly why Gate 1 divides the flow by turnover — the demand-relevant number is the stock, not the throughput. As a stock: the cargo model implies tens of billions held in place at every moment; the toll model requires a working balance big enough to cover the next fee. Either way you cut it, the two models are not on the same axis — which is the point. Staking and collateral demand, where they exist, sit in the deposit column and have to be sized separately.

Gate 2 is the other half. Gate 1 says how much value the float must hold. Gate 2 — the square-root market-impact law from Part I — says what price is required for large payments to clear through that float without moving the market. The intuition: the deeper the market, the smaller a fraction of it any single payment represents, and the less the payment moves the price; the square-root law makes that relationship precise. Part I and Field Note No. 19 run it in full; this note only needs Gate 1 to show the gap between cargo and toll.

XRP and XDC: same capability, different wiring

Both coins can carry native value. The difference is that XRP has currency bridging built into the ledger, while XDC's trade-finance adoption does not require XDC to carry the principal.

$500 million moving through XRP. If the payment routes through XRP, there must be enough XRP liquidity in place to absorb it. The questions become how much float is needed, how long it is held, and how often it is reused.

$500 million of trade receivables on XDC. The receivables move as their own tokens and XDC pays gas. XDC demand grows beyond fees only if something else happens: institutions hold XDC inventory, post it as collateral, stake it as validators, or choose it as the payment asset or the DvP cash leg. The settlement can be atomic on XDC either way — the question is only what plays the cash leg.

That does not rule out XDC appreciating. It means the XDC case needs evidence of one of those extra mechanisms before the toll label comes off — the same standard this series applies to XRP through the tripwires below.

Concessions at full strength

Two objections to this map deserve a fair hearing, and both get one.

First: XRP's bridge is optional. Nothing forces a bank or a payment company to route through XRP. They can move issued dollar tokens directly on the XRP Ledger, or on any other chain, and XRP never touches the principal. If that becomes the dominant habit, XRP sits much closer to the toll column than its design suggests.

Second: ETH and SOL already carry principal inside DEX routes. Many token-to-token swaps on Uniswap or Jupiter hop through WETH or SOL on the way (Uniswap routing). In those moments, the coin is cargo — and the map's "only for its own transfers" label deserves that asterisk. The differences are real but narrower than maximalists on either side would like: the routing is chosen per-trade at the application layer rather than built into the protocol's settlement path, it serves on-venue swaps rather than cross-currency payments, and routers increasingly prefer stablecoin legs. If app-level routing through a native coin hardens into a durable institutional habit, that coin earns a place in the cargo column — the same evidentiary standard XRP is held to here.

The series' structural answer to the first objection is a claim about efficiency, not impossibility. Direct settlement assets have to exist pair by pair: N currencies generate N×(N−1)/2 corridors, each funded in size on both sides, while a single bridge asset needs only N pairs — thirty currencies means 435 funded bilateral corridors, or thirty pairs against one bridge. Institutions already know this arithmetic, which is why global FX hubs through the dollar today — and the dollar's hub role is exactly the pre-funded correspondent structure whose costs this series started with. Field Note No. 16 ran the supply side of the argument: today's stablecoin float falls short of institutional atomic settlement not marginally but by an order of magnitude, and it is not even dedicated to this one use. This note deliberately grants the stronger version of the objection anyway — assume the float scales, because dollar stablecoins, tokenized deposits, and centralized liquidity hubs are scaling now, and hub-and-spoke designs genuinely cut the pair count without any neutral bridge. The cargo case should not depend on competitors staying small. Even granting scale, the question that remains is whether maintaining those funded balances across currencies, counterparties, and venues stays cheaper than routing through one deep, neutral intermediate. That is a question about comparative liquidity efficiency, and it will be answered by actual institutional routing behavior, not by design diagrams. The tripwires below are how we check what the routing behavior actually says.

How to read any adoption headline

Ask three questions, in order:

  1. How much value can this network move? That sizes the opportunity. It says nothing yet about the token.
  2. How much of that value must pass through, or sit in, the token? This is the cargo question. A toll answer here caps the upside no matter how big question 1 gets.
  3. How long does the token stay held? Tokens locked as float, reserves, or collateral press on the market differently than tokens bought for a fee and sold minutes later.

The most promising token economics are not the ones attached to the biggest headlines. They are the ones where real use creates sustained demand for the token itself.

What would change this note

For XRP, the cargo case is confirmed or weakened by tripwires the series tracks quarterly. They come in three kinds, and the order matters.

Direct cargo evidence — is XRP actually carrying principal?
Documented XRP-bridged payment volume: payment principal verifiably traveling through XRP. This is the direct measure of the thesis.
The auto-bridged share of token-to-token DEX trades (Observatory No. 4's live metric): a narrower, adjacent measure — XRP participation in exchange execution, where the hub mechanism is hard-coded. It tests the mechanism, not the payment flow.
Named institutional corridors with verifiable XRP participation, rather than RLUSD-only rails.
Liquidity capacity — could it carry more if chosen? (Gate 2's input)
Order-book and AMM depth on and off the ledger, including third-party DEX inventory.
Executable ticket size at institutional slippage tolerances, and market-maker inventories held rather than sourced just-in-time.
Supply conditions — what is the float doing? (Gate 1's denominator)
Exchange balances and long-term holdings.
Escrow re-locks or burns, and XRP locked as collateral in economically meaningful arrangements.

The first group is the thesis; the second and third are its preconditions. RLUSD migrating onto the XRP Ledger, or escrow re-locks, move the preconditions — only the first group shows the cargo actually moving. For any toll-model network, the equivalent tripwire is evidence that institutions are holding the native coin as inventory or collateral, not just spending it on fees. A headline without one of these is a network headline, not a token headline.

A follow-up note will apply that test partnership by partnership — for XRP, XDC, XLM, HBAR, LINK, and QNT, sorting each major announcement into requires the token, uses the technology only, or token role unproven.

The toll and the cargo

A trillion dollars can travel across a blockchain without creating a trillion dollars of demand for its native coin. That is the distinction this Field Note was written to establish.

For some networks, the token collects the toll. For others, it helps secure the road. For a smaller category, the token can become the cargo itself. But even carrying the cargo guarantees nothing: the asset must be chosen, the liquidity must exist, and holding that liquidity must pay.

The future of utility tokens will not be decided by how much business their networks attract. It will be decided by how much of that business their tokens actually capture. The highway can become indispensable without making any toll booth valuable. The question underneath all of it is whether institutions need to own the cargo to keep their money moving.
