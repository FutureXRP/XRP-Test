The blank spot on the map.

They drew the future of money. They left one thing off it.

Editor's note — what this is

This note reads one document in plain English: the Bank for International Settlements' big annual report, and specifically its chapter on the future of money, published in June 2026. The BIS is the institution central banks use the way you and I use a bank. When it publishes this report, central banks read it to decide what to build next. So this is as close to an official blueprint as the monetary world produces.

The argument is simple enough to say in one breath: the blueprint admits the future of money will be a bunch of separate digital ledgers that have to connect to each other. It describes, in detail, how badly things go when they can't connect. And it never names the thing that does the connecting. That blank spot is the whole note.

One honest caveat, stated once and never walked back: the report does not say "XRP." Not once. Everything here is reading the map, not quoting it — the same move Field Note 14 made across a stack of official documents that all describe the problem and none name the asset. And this is not a cheerful document for XRP holders. It's a central-bank report arguing for a central-bank-run future, and it's tougher on the alternatives than any version before it. The point is only about the one piece it leaves out.

Every June, the BIS publishes a flagship report, and one chapter of it tells the world's central banks where money is going next. This year's chapter is the clearest map they've drawn. It says where money is headed. It admits what that future will actually look like. And then it describes the destination in six words worth holding onto.

"A system of fully interoperable ledgers."

— BIS Annual Economic Report 2026, on how the future system may be built

Read that slowly, because it's the whole game. The most buttoned-up institution in finance just said the future of money will not be one big system. It will be many separate ledgers — separate digital account books — that have to be wired together. Plural. Not one. The world of scattered, disconnected ledgers that this series has argued was coming isn't a prediction we have to defend anymore. It's now the official plan's own starting assumption. Everything below comes from simply taking them at their word.

Section 01 — The map they drew

First, an honest word about what kind of document this is, because the case is stronger when you describe it straight. This is not a friendly report. Its whole spirit is: keep the system anchored to central banks, build our own upgraded version, and keep stablecoins on a short leash. Compared to last year, the official plan got firmer, not softer. A shared cross-border test the central banks have been running is now reported as a working prototype rather than a someday idea. Stablecoins get formally demoted — the report says today's versions look more like investment funds than like real money.

So the opening this note is about isn't a crack in the argument. It's a blank space on an otherwise crowded map. And it happens to be the exact blank space this series has been pointing at since Part I.

Section 02 — The blank spot

Here's the strange part. The report describes a future of many separate ledgers, and it knows those ledgers need to connect. It even gives the connection a name — it calls interoperability money's "connective tissue." It then spends pages on what goes wrong when the tissue isn't there: send money from one ledger to another that doesn't speak the same language, and it can simply vanish. The report uses those exact words — permanent loss.

So it names the future shape. It names the connecting problem. It names the danger. It names everything — except the thing that goes in the middle and does the connecting. Line up what the report says out loud against what it never says, and the hole is obvious.

That left-hand column is a precise description of a job. The right-hand column is the worker who does it, left unnamed on every line. The rest of this note is just walking the three biggest reasons that blank spot exists — and why the same shape keeps appearing.

Section 03 — Seven roads out of a hundred and sixty

The report's proof that any of this can work is a project called Agorá. Seven central banks and more than forty big private banks built a shared pipe to move money between them. And it works — real money, settled instantly, across currencies. Genuine achievement. This note doesn't dispute it.

But count the countries. Seven central banks means seven currencies. Now look at the wider world: roughly 160 currencies are in everyday use. The seven biggest get a gleaming new pipe. The other 150-plus get nothing — and those leftover routes are exactly where moving money today is slowest and most expensive. Think Nigeria to the Philippines, or Mexico to Korea: the corridors the big banks have no commercial reason to wire.

The rich-country core is building itself a private highway. That was never the question. The question is every road the highway doesn't reach — and that's most of the map.

This is where the argument has to stay disciplined, so here's the careful version. The strong claim is small and holds up: a club of the biggest central banks will wire themselves together and won't bother wiring the long tail, because nobody in the club gets paid to. The weak claim — that XRP "replaces the whole system" — does not hold up, and the report itself hands critics the ammunition to knock it down. The connector's job was never to beat the rich-country highway. Its job is the 150 roads the highway skips.

Section 04 — Open to use, orderly to run

The report's big dividing line is "public" networks versus "private" ones. Public networks — like Ethereum — are open to anyone. The report says they're a mess: tolls to use them, traffic jams when they get busy, fees that spike. So, it concludes, the future has to be private and invite-only.

But that mashes two different questions into one. Question one: who's allowed to keep the books? Question two: who's allowed to use the system? The report's complaints — the tolls, the jams — are all about how the books get kept on those public networks. They have nothing to do with who's allowed in the door.

And there's a design that keeps its books in an orderly way — a known, named set of bookkeepers, no tolls, no traffic jams — while still letting anyone use it. The report lists every other kind of network in a neat table. That one is missing from the table entirely.

Look at the bottom row. The XRP Ledger keeps its books with a known, named group, charges no tolls, doesn't jam — and still lets anyone use it. It's the only row that's orderly to run and open to use. And it's the only one the report leaves off its list. The report's whole case against public networks is aimed at the tolls and traffic of letting anyone keep the books. It simply never describes a network that's open to use but tidy about who keeps the books. That design isn't argued against. It's just not on the page.

Why does "open to use" matter so much? Because an invite-only system needs someone to send the invitations — and at the global level, that someone is the club of big central banks. An open system is the only kind where the 150 left-out countries don't have to wait for an invitation. Open access isn't a detail. It's the difference between reaching most of the world and reaching seven currencies.

One honest point in the other direction, because this is where the argument is most exposed. The XRP Ledger's main record is public — anyone can see the transactions — and big institutions often don't want their money flows on display. The report flags exactly that concern about public networks, and it's a real one. The fair answer is that privacy can be handled in the layers built on top, not baked into the base. But it's the soft spot, and it's worth naming as one rather than pretending it away.

Section 05 — The swap that only works in one room

The report's dream is a clean swap: two things change hands at the very same instant, or neither does — so no one can hand over their side and get stiffed on the other. XRP people will recognize this; the series has called it atomic settlement. It's the holy grail of moving money, and the report is right to want it.

But here's the quiet catch. That instant swap only works when both things are sitting in the same room — on the same ledger. The report imagines three different versions of a stablecoin future, and every single one of them happens inside one connected system. One room. Watch.

The three futures the report imagines — and the one it never does
Version 1 — the coin is backed by bank deposits
Money shuffles from one kind of bank account to another, all inside the same banking system. Everyone's in the same room.
Happens inside one system
Version 2 — the coin is backed by government IOUs
The coin issuer buys short-term government debt instead. Different asset, same single country, same connected set of books. Still one room.
Happens inside one system
Version 3 — the coin is backed by central-bank money
The safest version: the backing sits right at the central bank. One central bank, one system, one room.
Happens inside one system
The version the report never describes — two different rooms
Two separate ledgers. No shared central bank. No matching asset waiting on the other side. And value that has to cross the hallway between them safely, all-or-nothing, with nobody getting burned. Carrying value across that hallway is the connector's entire job. The report models it nowhere.
✓ This is the connector's job — and the blueprint skips it
The report works out everything that happens inside a room. It says nothing about what carries value between rooms. That's not a mistake to laugh at — it's the exact shape of the gap. The instant swap they love only works when both sides share a room. The connector is for everyone who doesn't.

So the claim splits cleanly in two, and only the second part is about the token. First, the XRP Ledger can do the report's own instant swap for two things that live on it — same prize, on a network that's open to everyone instead of invite-only. Second, and this is where XRP itself earns its keep: it's the carrier in the hallway, for all the routes where there's no matching asset waiting on the far side. The honest limit: when both sides already share a room, you don't need a carrier — they just swap directly. The connector's whole reason to exist is the space between systems, not the dense middle where the big players already sit together.

Section 06 — Where this could be wrong

This series doesn't get to claim a clean win and walk off. Here, plainly, is what would break the argument — and you should hold these as live risks, not rhetorical throat-clearing.

It breaks if the central banks' own "connect the ledgers" effort ends up wiring the long tail itself, through some invite-only bridge, before any neutral carrier gets the chance. It breaks if a government-issued digital currency, shared across enough countries, grows to cover the routes that actually matter — shrinking the leftover space to nothing worth pricing. And it's weakest at the report's sharpest punch: on an open network, it's hard to be sure who you're dealing with, which is a real problem for banks that have to know their customers. The fair answer is that you check IDs at the on-ramps and off-ramps — the regulated doors where people enter and exit — rather than on the highway itself. That's a genuine answer, but it's the place the open-network case is most exposed, and it should stay flagged as such.

None of those have happened. All of them could. The argument is conditional on them not resolving the wrong way — and the report, read honestly, doesn't resolve them the wrong way. It describes the exact world where the connector is needed, and declines to name it.

What to hold from this note

The whole thing, in five lines
The blueprint got firmer, not friendlier. The central banks' shared test is now a working prototype, and stablecoins got demoted to "looks like an investment fund, not money." This is not a document cheering for XRP. Say that plainly and the blank spot it leaves is more believable, not less.
It admits the future is many ledgers, not one. In its own words, "a system of fully interoperable ledgers." It calls the connection between them money's "connective tissue," and warns that money sent between ledgers can be lost forever. Then it never names the connector.

There's a design that's orderly to run and open to everyone — and it's missing from their list. The report's complaints about public networks are about tolls and traffic, not about who's allowed in. The one network that's tidy and open at once is the only one left off the page.

The blueprint names every part of the machine but one. The gap is real, the risks that could close it are real, and the outcome is conditional. The chair is still empty — now in the most official description of the room anyone has written. What the seat is worth, and the price at which XRP's market is deep enough to sit in it, is the calculation Part I and Field Note 5 run on the square-root depth formula. This note only confirms the seat exists — and the blueprint just did that for us.

The careful distinction: The instant swap the report demonstrates across borders is a cash-for-cash swap. That's related to, but not the same as, the asset-for-cash swap Field Note 13 showed can't be split. Both are "same room" swaps — which is the point here.
