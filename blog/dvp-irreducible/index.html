Editor's note — framework status

This field note contains the most significant analytical finding in the series to date. It is not a price prediction. It is a structural argument sourced from primary regulatory and institutional authorities that changes which pricing model is binding — and therefore changes how the series' scenarios should be read.

Two things happen in this note. First, a refinement: most real-world DvP settlement will use conditional escrow rather than pure atomic batch, and those flows are primarily priced via MV=PQ rather than the square root law. This moderates some of the series' higher price scenarios. Second, and more important: a narrower class of transactions may require true simultaneous atomic execution rather than escrow-style conditional release — either because the legal instrument, operational standard, or counterparty risk policy does not permit a meaningful exposure window between legs. For those transactions, the square root law is the applicable model, and it is set by peak ticket size rather than annual volume.

These two findings work in opposite directions and must be understood together.

Section 01 — What DvP actually means

Delivery versus payment is not a technology preference. It is a legal and operational structure developed specifically to eliminate principal risk — the risk that one party delivers securities without receiving payment, or vice versa.

The Bank for International Settlements defined the standard in 1992 in its report Delivery Versus Payment in Securities Settlement Systems. The Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures (PFMI), jointly published by BIS and IOSCO and adopted as regulatory standard by all major jurisdictions, elevated it to a mandatory component of resilient financial market infrastructure. The New York Federal Reserve's own language is unambiguous: delivery occurs if and only if payment occurs.

All three BIS DvP models — gross real-time, securities gross with end-of-day funds netting, and both legs netted at end of day — preserve this linkage without exception. In none of the three models can the legs be decoupled.

One important qualification: some advanced DvP systems — notably TARGET2-Securities (T2S), the ECB's pan-European settlement platform covering 23 countries — operate optional partial settlement windows, running five times daily. T2S partial settlement allows a transaction to settle in a smaller amount when the full securities quantity is unavailable, but it does so under strict proportionality rules: the cash leg moves in exact proportion to the securities actually delivered, and the DvP linkage is preserved on whatever portion settles. This is a feature layered on top of DvP, not a violation of it — it does not permit the cash leg to settle independently of the delivery leg, or vice versa. The "if and only if" property is maintained proportionally. The irreducibility argument in Section 04 applies to the minimum floor size set by the instrument's contractual terms — not to an absolute prohibition of partial settlement in every system under every circumstance.

The mechanism that enforces the "if and only if" property is atomicity. In computer science terms: the transaction either succeeds completely or fails completely, with no intermediate state. Partial completion is not an outcome. If any leg fails, the entire transaction reverts as if it never happened.

On the XRP Ledger specifically, the BatchV1_1 amendment — the corrected replacement for XLS-56 — is designed to implement this mechanism via ALLORNOTHING mode. Up to eight inner transactions execute as a single indivisible outer transaction. All succeed, or the entire batch fails and reverts. There is no partial settlement. The cap of eight inner transactions is a real constraint: simple 2–3 leg DvP (the repo and tokenized securities use cases this note focuses on) fits comfortably within it; complex multi-party netting chains would require either multiple batches or the escrow mechanism.

Protocol status — February 2026

The original Batch amendment (XLS-56) was disabled in rippled v3.1.1 on February 23, 2026, following responsible disclosure of a critical signature-validation bug by security researcher Pranamya Keshkamat and Cantina AI's autonomous tool Apex. The bug would have allowed an attacker to execute inner transactions on behalf of arbitrary victim accounts without their private keys. Critically, the amendment had not activated on mainnet — it was still in the validator voting phase — so no funds were ever at risk.

The emergency disable was a preventative measure, not damage control, and reflects the system working as designed. The corrected replacement, BatchV1_1, has been implemented and is currently under security review with no confirmed release date. In the interim, Token Escrow (XLS-85, live on mainnet since February 2026) is the operational DvP protection mechanism — which reinforces the escrow-dominant scenario modeled in Section 03.

IMF Fintech Note 2025/011 — Strict vs. weak atomicity · November 2025

Strict atomicity — the gold standard — is only guaranteed when both assets and payments are recorded and settled on a single ledger with a unified execution environment. Smart contracts enforce delivery and payment simultaneously, with finality provided by the consensus mechanism rather than by legal or institutional arrangements. For same-ledger XRPL-native DvP, BatchV1_1 provides strict atomicity.

Weak atomicity — achievable cross-ledger — uses hash-time-locked contracts (HTLCs) that coordinate execution by acting as technical escrows, releasing transfers only if cryptographic conditions are met within a time window. Token Escrow with crypto-conditions operates in this regime for cross-ledger DvP (securities on Canton, cash leg on XRPL). The IMF states explicitly: "Cross-ledger arrangements cannot replicate the guarantees of strict atomicity achievable under a single ledger environment." Both eliminate principal risk. Only strict atomicity provides the technical guarantee without any reliance on legal or institutional enforcement — making BatchV1_1 the correct mechanism for the highest-value, most risk-sensitive DvP transactions, not merely an enhancement of Token Escrow.

Source: IMF Fintech Note 2025/011, Central Bank Exploration of Tokenized Reserves (Kunaratskul, Lannquist, Reslow, Zhang), Box 1 — Atomicity and Atomic Settlement.

NY Fed Liberty Street Economics — Simultaneous vs. instant settlement · November 2022

Lee, Martin, and Müller (NY Fed, 2022) separate two distinct properties that "atomic settlement" combines: simultaneous settlement (the "if and only if" property — one leg conditional on the other, achievable through both atomic execution and conditional escrow) and instant settlement (immediate finality in the same ledger close, with no delay between trade agreement and settlement). Token Escrow provides simultaneous settlement without instant settlement. BatchV1_1 provides both in a single 3–5 second ledger close.

The NY Fed confirms the liquidity burden of instant settlement: traders can only sell securities they already hold, requiring pre-positioned liquidity. This is precisely the depth requirement the series models through the square root law. The Talos empirical model (Section below) validates this further: atomic DvP — with no VWAP spreading available, the full transaction executing in a single ledger close — requires more instantaneous depth than equivalent algorithmically executed orders of the same size.

Any mechanism that allows the cash and delivery legs to settle at different times, in different amounts, or under different conditions has ceased to be true simultaneous atomic DvP. It has reintroduced exactly the principal risk that DvP was designed to eliminate. Conditional escrow mechanisms — which enforce the same "if and only if" protection through locked pre-commitment rather than simultaneous transfer — preserve DvP's core guarantee through different means. Section 03 addresses this directly.

Section 02 — The splitting question, answered precisely

The community debate around Prisma — Ripple's proprietary liquidity aggregation API confirmed by David Schwartz — centers on whether XRP's transaction-splitting capabilities resolve the depth requirement the series models. The answer is: for PvP flows, partially. For DvP flows, not at all.

Prisma routes large PvP (payment versus payment) transactions through multiple exchanges and DEX liquidity simultaneously, breaking them into smaller sub-transactions distributed over time — TWAP-style execution applied to settlement. This is a genuine capability and it does reduce the peak depth required for PvP flows. The two blockers Schwartz named when describing Prisma — robust stablecoins in major currencies and legal clarity — have both materially improved with RLUSD approaching institutional scale and the SEC case resolved.

For DvP, Prisma's splitting mechanic is inapplicable by definition. Here is the precise reason: if you split a DvP transaction — delivering half the securities against half the cash first, then the other half — you have created two sequential transfers. Between the first settlement and the second, principal risk exists. The first half settled but the second half might not. That exposure window is the exact risk DvP was invented to eliminate.

DvP cannot be leg-decoupled. Splitting a DvP into sequential one-sided transfers destroys the "if and only if" property. What DvP systems like T2S permit is proportional partial DvP, where both legs settle in reduced but still linked quantities. That is a refinement of DvP, not a decoupling of it.

This is not a limitation of Prisma specifically. It is a constraint that applies to every conceivable routing architecture. The atomicity that eliminates principal risk is the same property that prohibits leg-decoupled partial execution. They are the same thing. You cannot have one without the other.

Section 03 — The escrow distinction: most DvP will not be atomic

Here is the refinement that moderates some of the series' higher scenarios.

Token Escrow (XLS-85) went live on XRPL mainnet in February 2026. It extends native escrow to all issued tokens — IOUs and Multi-Purpose Tokens — not just XRP. The mechanism: the cash leg (XRP or RLUSD) is locked into an on-ledger escrow contract. Release occurs only when pre-defined conditions are met — cryptographic proof of delivery, oracle confirmation, or custom logic via Smart Escrows launching Q2 2026.

This achieves conditional DvP protection — the same "if and only if" guarantee — without requiring both legs to execute in the same ledger close. The security mechanism is conditional locking rather than simultaneous transfer. Principal risk is eliminated: neither party can run with the asset while the other waits. The result is functionally equivalent to DvP's core guarantee, delivered through pre-commitment rather than simultaneity. This is why Token Escrow should be understood as an alternative implementation of DvP's protection, not a relaxation of it.

Section 05 — What this means for the pricing model

The series has used the square root law throughout as the primary pricing constraint. The escrow/atomic split insight refines when each model applies — it does not eliminate the square root law from the framework.

For the escrow-dominant majority, MV=PQ is the right primary tool. The series' velocity equation, applied to annual flow volume across all three layers — DTCC residual, non-dollar FX corridors, derivatives margin — produces base case implied prices of $300–$600 at conservative adoption assumptions and expanded float.

For the atomic-batch minority, the square root law sets a floor that is determined by the largest single irreducible transaction. This floor is independent of annual volume. A market could process only ten atomic DvP transactions per year — but if any one of them is a $2B block trade in tokenized Treasuries, the market must have depth to handle $2B atomically at institutional slippage tolerance. Annual flow is irrelevant. The peak ticket is everything.

The two constraints must both be satisfied simultaneously — and in the full integration scenario with large-ticket atomic settlement, the square root law becomes binding even if it governs a minority of transactions by count or notional.

One layer of a three-layer model. Before reading the pricing outputs below, one structural fact must be stated plainly. The pricing analysis in this note addresses a single demand pool: DvP settlement transactions that require true atomic or conditionally atomic execution — the DTCC cross-border residual and non-dollar corridor DvP flows. This is not the complete pricing picture. The series models three separate and additive demand pools on the same fixed 6 billion XRP productive float: Layer 1 — the DTCC repo and Treasury clearing residual (this note and Observatory No. 3); Layer 2 — the global non-dollar FX settlement corridor market, $150–300 trillion annually outside CLS and SWIFT (Observatory No. 3 and Field Note 14); Layer 3 — the global derivatives margin layer (Part V). Each layer competes for the same productive float simultaneously. The prices derived in this note are the floor implied by DvP adoption alone at corridor-specific slippage tolerances. If all three layers develop, they are additive on the same supply — and the compounding effect is the central analytical contribution of the full series. The ceiling is determined by how many of the three additive demand pools materialize simultaneously. These numbers are the floor, not the ceiling.

The most important thing to understand about the pricing output is that it is not one number — it is a range produced by six inputs, and one of those inputs dwarfs all the others combined. That input is slippage tolerance. At 3.5 basis points — the tightest institutional standard, applicable to sovereign-scale FX settlement where 1% slippage on a $50B transaction costs $500M and eliminates the efficiency argument entirely — the square root law produces implied prices in the hundreds to thousands of dollars per XRP for large institutional tickets. At 50 basis points — the outer bound for a disintermediated market where intermediaries and their price-smoothing function have been removed — the same model produces prices in the tens to low hundreds. That is a 200-fold difference from changing one input. Every other variable in the model moves the needle by factors of 2 to 5. Slippage tolerance moves it by 200. The honest answer is that the real-world tolerance for institutional DvP settlement on XRPL has never been observed, because the system has never operated at institutional scale. The series will update the scenarios when observable data exists.

The second most important input is productive float — the XRP actually available for settlement use rather than sitting in dormant wallets, ETF cold storage, or long-term holdings. Today that figure is approximately 6 billion tokens. As price rises, LP yield incentives pull dormant supply into active circulation — market makers, AMM pools, settlement inventory. At 20 billion tokens in active use (roughly 33% of circulating supply), MV=PQ implied prices fall by a factor of 3.3 relative to the 6B baseline. This is a moderating force on the high-end scenarios, not a reason to dismiss them. Higher float means the same monetary base is distributed across more tokens, which lowers the per-token price required to support a given volume. The direction is honest: the series' highest price scenarios assume the productive float stays constrained. As adoption expands the float, the required price per token falls — though the required total market cap does not.

The escrow/atomic split — provisionally modeled at 70/30 — is the third significant input and the most uncertain. Moving from 80/20 escrow-to-atomic to 50/50 increases the blended implied price by 40–50% because a larger share of volume falls under the square root law rather than MV=PQ. Whether institutional adoption tilts toward escrow-dominant conditional settlement or toward true atomic batch depends on how quickly BatchV1_1 activates, how regulators treat escrow-based finality versus simultaneous settlement, and what counterparty risk policies the first institutional adopters bring to the table. The series treats this split as a sensitivity variable, not a prediction. Velocity and market share capture round out the remaining inputs — each moving the MV=PQ component by factors of 2 to 3, significant in isolation but secondary to the slippage and float questions.

One additional friction cost belongs in any honest treatment: acquisition slippage. The slippage tolerance modeled applies to the DvP execution leg only — the moment XRP moves as the cash leg of a settlement transaction. In most institutional workflows, XRP must first be acquired via a stablecoin or fiat conversion immediately before the transaction executes. That pre-positioning step creates its own market impact, additive to the execution slippage. The effective tolerance available for the DvP leg is therefore lower than the institutional benchmark implies — some portion of the tolerance budget is consumed before settlement begins. This does not change the pricing logic, but it is a real friction that institutional treasury desks will price into their settlement economics.

What does the blended model actually produce? Using the 70/30 provisional split, 6–20 billion in productive float depending on adoption stage, and conservative market share assumptions across the three layers — DTCC residual, non-dollar FX corridors, derivatives margin — the implied range is approximately $280 to $1,100. At 20 billion productive float with the 70/30 split, independent modeling converges on a central case of approximately $1,050–$1,100. These are not predictions. They are the arithmetic output of stated inputs applied to a framework that has been stress-tested across five review cycles. Change any input and the outputs change. Section 06 — The competitive landscape through this lens

TARGET2-Securities — the ECB's pan-European settlement platform covering 23 countries and settling in central bank money — is the world's most advanced DvP infrastructure in terms of participant count and cross-border scope. It is also jurisdiction-specific: it covers Eurozone and select EU nations using central bank money in euros or Danish krone. An Asian institution, a Gulf sovereign wealth fund, or an institution settling cross-currency between non-aligned blocs faces a gap T2S does not address. This is not a criticism of T2S — it is exactly the right design for its purpose. It is evidence that the cross-jurisdictional non-dollar DvP gap is structural, not a failure of existing infrastructure to solve a problem it was designed to solve.

JPMorgan Kinexys has broken out of its original layer-lock — it now connects to Canton Network and public blockchain infrastructure, and JPM Coin is explicitly designed to be offered on external blockchains. The technical layer-lock argument should be retired. However, the jurisdictional argument is unchanged and is in fact strengthened by Kinexys's expansion. JPM Coin is a claim on JPMorgan Chase deposits. JPMorgan Chase is a U.S.-chartered bank subject to U.S. jurisdiction, OFAC enforcement, and Treasury oversight. A foreign institutional counterparty that routes DvP settlement through JPM Coin is placing its settlement activity inside the U.S. legal perimeter. This is a demonstrated, exercised power — used in living institutional memory against Russia in 2022 — that risk management teams in Beijing, New Delhi, Riyadh, and Jakarta are explicitly required to account for.

Regional stablecoins (EURC, JPY stablecoins, CNH instruments) solve the same-currency leg efficiently. They do not solve the cross-currency non-dollar DvP cash leg — the yen-to-euro, sterling-to-won, real-to-dirham transaction where neither counterparty wants to convert through a dollar instrument at all. XRP and XLM are among the few credible permissionless, trustless, geopolitically neutral candidates designed for this kind of bridge function. Every other widely deployed alternative has counterparty or jurisdictional trust embedded in its structure.

Dollar-denominated DvP: RLUSD first, XRP as overflow bridge. Most cross-border DvP settlement in Treasury and repo markets is dollar-denominated — a U.S. Treasury security settling against dollars, even if the counterparty is a Japanese pension fund or a Gulf sovereign wealth fund. For those transactions, the cash leg obligation is in dollars. RLUSD — a dollar stablecoin on XRPL — satisfies that obligation directly, without the two-hop FX conversion that XRP would require. Where RLUSD supply is sufficient to meet instantaneous atomic DvP depth requirements, RLUSD is the structurally correct instrument for dollar-denominated cross-border DvP.

However, RLUSD supply is currently approximately $1.55 billion. The dollar-denominated cross-border Treasury clearing market the TBAC describes runs $926 billion daily in cash transactions and $2.4 trillion daily in uncleared bilateral repo. Even at V=50× annual velocity, $1.55 billion in RLUSD supports approximately $310 million in daily settlement flow — a fraction of the market. At current supply, RLUSD cannot serve as the primary settlement asset for the dollar corridor at institutional scale. For dollar-denominated flows that exceed RLUSD's available depth, XRP serves as an ODL-style just-in-time bridge: dollars to XRP to dollars, atomically, within a single settlement window. This is not a design preference — it is a supply constraint. XRP's role in dollar corridors therefore shrinks as RLUSD scales and grows as institutional clearing volumes expand faster than RLUSD supply. The instrument separation is directionally correct — RLUSD is designed for dollar corridors, XRP for non-dollar corridors — but XRP absorbs the dollar corridor overflow for as long as RLUSD supply is insufficient to meet instantaneous institutional DvP depth requirements. Both instruments expand XRP's addressable market rather than competing for the same flows. The DvP depth argument the square root law models applies most cleanly to XRP in the non-dollar cross-currency corridor. It applies secondarily to XRP as a dollar corridor overflow bridge for as long as RLUSD supply is constrained.

A critical objection must be stated honestly: a thriving XRPL does not automatically translate into proportional demand for XRP. If stablecoins become the main unit of account on XRPL — RLUSD for dollar corridors, EURC for euro corridors — then stablecoin-to-stablecoin routing can expand without forcing participants to hold much XRP beyond protocol fees and reserves. In that scenario, XRPL succeeds while XRP remains optional.

The pricing thesis in this note depends on XRP being the bridge asset specifically — not merely a token used on XRPL. The case rests on the sovereignty and non-dollar corridor argument developed in Observatory No. 3: for cross-currency DvP between non-aligned jurisdictions, RLUSD is a dollar instrument, regional stablecoins are sovereign instruments, and only a permissionless non-sovereign bridge satisfies the jurisdictional neutrality requirement. The DvP pricing argument is only as strong as the non-dollar corridor argument that underlies it.

The atomic DvP segment is the highest-value, highest-depth-requirement segment — where XRP's structural advantages are most acute and where the square root law produces its most demanding price floors. But that advantage only materializes in corridors where institutional counterparties have jurisdictional reasons to avoid dollar-denominated alternatives. Sizing the market requires sizing those specific corridors.
